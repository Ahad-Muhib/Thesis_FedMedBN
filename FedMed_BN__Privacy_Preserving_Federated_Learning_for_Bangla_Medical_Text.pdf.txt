Abstract
Sharing medical data across hospitals could unlock huge advances in healthcare AI, but
it comes with a real problem: patient records are sensitive, and no institution can simply
hand over raw data to build a shared model. This tension between collaboration and
privacy is exactly what motivated this work. To navigate it, we turned to Federated
Learning (FL), an approach that lets multiple hospitals train a shared model together
while each one keeps its own patient data locally, never sending it anywhere.
We applied this idea to Bangla medical text, a space that has been largely overlooked.
While English has been the focus of most medical NLP research, with vast datasets and
pretrained models readily available, Bangla has not received the same attention, despite
being spoken by hundreds of millions of people. This gap means that many of the tools
clinicians and researchers rely on in English simply don’t exist for Bangla, and patients
whose records are written in Bangla are effectively left out of these advances. Motivated
by this gap, we developed FedMed-BN, a privacy-preserving federated learning frame-
work built specifically for Bangla medical text.
We see this work as more than a technical exercise. By bringing together healthcare
NLP and federated learning in a language that has been underserved, FedMed-BN aims
to be a genuine step toward more inclusive medical AI, one that could eventually support
better prescription analysis, disease classification, and clinical documentation tools for
Bangla-speaking communities, without asking hospitals to compromise on patient privacy
to get there.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Background and Motivation
In Bangladesh, the swift digitalization of healthcare has resulted in an explosion of un-
structured clinical text in Bangla, including prescriptions, symptom descriptions, dis-
charge summaries and electronic health record (EHR) notes. Despite this demographic
scale, Bangla is significantly underrepresented in the medical NLP literature, a disparity
that remains even when compared to languages with much smaller speaker populations
but more established clinical NLP infrastructures [20]. This underrepresentation is fur-
ther aggravated by a structural constraint inherent to the Bangladeshi healthcare system.
Clinical data is dispersed across hospitals, diagnostic chains and specialist clinics which
operate as isolated institutional silos due to confidentiality obligations, patient privacy
considerations and the absence of a unified national health data policy. Consequently,
centralized aggregation of such data for model training is rendered both ethically un-
tenable and practically infeasible, rendering conventional deep learning pipelines - which
presuppose centralized data access-fundamentally incompatible with the operational re-
alities of Bangladeshi clinical informatics. This study is motivated by the imperative to
address this dual gap by developing high-performance Bangla clinical NLP methods that
obviate the need for raw patient data to leave its institutional source [9].
1.2 Problem Statement
The primary concern that this work addresses is the simultaneous occurrence of three
connected issues, namely, the lack of effective Bangla-specific clinical language models,
the inability to centralize sensitive patient data across institutions, and the vulnerabil-
1

--- Page 13 ---
ity of standard decentralized training approaches to adversarial reconstruction attacks,
known as gradient leakage. While current Federated Learning (FL) frameworks are de-
signed to avoid sharing raw data, advanced inference attacks have been shown to be able
to mathematically invert private text sequences from transmitted model weight updates
[15]. Thus, network isolation alone is insufficient to ensure the confidentiality of patients.
Taken together, these three issues define a problem that no existing approach fully re-
solves: an effective clinical NLP solution for Bangla must simultaneously (i) achieve
competitive performance on a morphologically rich, low-resource language, (ii) operate
under the institutional, legal, and ethical constraints that prevent centralized data pool-
ing in Bangladeshi healthcare, and (iii) provide a formal, quantifiable privacy guarantee
that holds even against adversaries with access to the shared model updates themselves
[21]. This research is positioned to address this composite problem directly, rather than
treating any one of its dimensions in isolation.
1.3 Objectives of the Project
To address these overlapping challenges, this research pursues the following objectives.
First, it establishes a Federated Learning framework specifically optimized for unstruc-
tured Bangla medical text, simulating a realistic multi-institutional hospital environment
using distributed data partitions. Second, the system utilizes a technique referred to
as differentially private federated averaging (DP-FedAvg), where calibrated Gaussian
noise is added to the gradients of the local models before their aggregates are per-
formed by the server. it fine-tunes a pre-trained Bangla transformer model-specifically
csebuetnlp/banglabert-for Bangla medical named entity recognition (NER), identifying
entities such as Disease, Medicine, Organ, Hormone, Pharmacological Class, and Com-
mon Medical Terms. Fourth, it evaluates model convergence and stability under Non-IID
data distributions, which arise naturally when different institutions exhibit different di-
agnostic patterns and documentation styles.

1.5 Scope of the Work
The work is restricted to Bangla medical named entity recognition (NER), performed
on Bangla-language clinical text drawn from the Bangla-MedER and BanglaBioMed cor-
pora. One pre-trained Bangla transformer backbone, csebuetnlp/banglabert, is fine-tuned
within the federated setting; the design and pre-training of these base models is outside
the scope of this work. The multi-institutional environment is simulated in software using
five distributed data partitions that emulate realistic, non-IID hospital data distributions;
the study does not involve deployment within actual hospital infrastructure or live pa-
tient data. Privacy protection is provided through DP-FedAvg, with the privacy budget
formalized using the (ε, δ)-differential privacy definition; other privacy-enhancing tech-
niques, such as secure multi-party computation or homomorphic encryption, are noted
in the literature but are not implemented here.
Finally, the study is limited to the
4

--- Page 16 ---
textual modality of clinical data; medical imaging, structured tabular EHR fields, and
speech-based clinical data are not considered.
1.6 Expected Outcomes
This research is expected to produce an end-to-end Federated Learning pipeline tailored
to Bangla medical text, in which differential privacy is incorporated as a first-class design
component rather than an afterthought. Unlike the overwhelming majority of existing
Bangla clinical NLP studies, which require all training data to be pooled in a single
location, the proposed framework is expected to demonstrate that collaborative model
training across five simulated hospital nodes is achievable without any raw text ever leav-
ing its local environment. The work is also expected to yield a structured experimental
benchmark built on established Bangla clinical corpora, including the Bangla-MedER
and BanglaBioMed datasets, partitioned under realistic Non-IID constraints to simulate
genuine institutional heterogeneity. A further expected outcome is a quantifiable and
auditable measure of patient data protection, expressed through the (ε, δ)-differential
privacy budget, that makes the trade-off between privacy and utility explicit and ad-
justable. Collectively, these outcomes are intended to establish a replicable blueprint
for privacy-preserving medical NLP in other low-resource linguistic contexts, with par-
ticular relevance for South and Southeast Asian healthcare systems facing similar data
fragmentation and linguistic diversity challenges.
1.7 Impacts of the Project
Beyond its technical contributions, this project carries implications that extend into so-
cietal, health and legal, and environmental domains. The following subsections outline
these broader impacts.
1.7.1 Impact on Societal and Cultural Issues
By targeting Bangla—a language spoken natively by over 170 million people in Bangladesh
and more than 230 million people worldwide—this project works toward closing a long-
standing technological gap that has left Bangla-speaking patients underserved by modern
clinical AI tools [11]. Improved Bangla clinical NLP capability can, over time, support
5

--- Page 17 ---
better documentation, retrieval, and decision-support tools for clinicians who work pri-
marily in Bangla, rather than forcing reliance on English-centric systems [24]. This has
a cultural dimension as well: it affirms that native-language clinical documentation does
not need to be a barrier to adopting advanced AI tools, and it contributes to the broader
movement toward linguistic inclusion in NLP research and digital health infrastructure
[30, 26].
1.7.2 Impact on Health, Safety, and Legal Issues
The federated, privacy-preserving design of this work is directly motivated by patient
safety and legal considerations. By ensuring that raw patient text never leaves its insti-
tutional source, and by formalizing a quantifiable (ε, δ) privacy budget, the framework is
intended to reduce the risk of patient re-identification or data exposure relative to con-
ventional centralized training pipelines [2]. This is particularly relevant in Bangladesh,
where institutional confidentiality obligations and patient privacy ethics already constrain
data sharing, even in the absence of a single comprehensive national health data policy.
At the same time, any system that processes drug names, dosages, and disease identi-
fiers carries health and safety responsibilities. The NER and classification capabilities
developed in this work are intended as decision-support aids that can assist clinicians
and researchers in structuring unstructured notes, not as an autonomous diagnostic au-
thority; outputs would require clinical validation before influencing patient care decisions
[25, 3]. Care has also been taken to evaluate model performance under realistic, non-IID
conditions, so that reported accuracy is not artificially inflated by favorable, idealized
data distributions.
1.7.3 Impact on Environment and Sustainability Issues
Training and fine-tuning transformer-based language models carries a computational,
and therefore energy, cost, and the federated setting used here introduces additional
communication overhead from repeated rounds of model-update exchange between sim-
ulated hospital nodes and the central server. This project mitigates some of this cost by
fine-tuning existing pre-trained Bangla transformer backbones rather than training large
models from scratch, and by working with modestly sized clinical corpora rather than
web-scale data.
6

--- Page 18 ---
More broadly, the federated approach itself has a sustainability rationale: because raw
data never has to be duplicated, transferred, and stored centrally, this design avoids the
storage and transmission overhead—and corresponding energy footprint—of large-scale
centralized data pooling [13, 8]. In this sense, the privacy-preserving architecture in-
vestigated in this work also aligns with the goal of building clinical AI infrastructure
that scales sustainably across multiple institutions, rather than depending on ever-larger
centralized repositories of sensitive data.

Chapter 2
Background and Literature Review
This chapter presents the theoretical foundation and reviews related research relevant
to our project on Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning for Bangla Medical Text. It
begins with essential concepts and definitions in the Preliminaries, followed by a detailed
survey of existing systems. The chapter then compares these methods, highlights their
limitations, and identifies research gaps addressed by our proposed system.
2.1 Preliminaries
In this chapter we outline the basic ideas that will allow us to use FedMed-BN.
Federated Learning (FL)
Federated Learning was introduced by Google [14] for supporting the data privacy-
sensitive character of distributed learning. It allows multiple clients to collaborate in
training a single model of shared knowledge, without having to share any client’s raw
data. Instead, each party trains the model individually on their own data and shares
only the changes they made to the model since the last round — called “updates” or
“gradients” — with the other parties. The changes are aggregated by the central server,
which then returns the updated global model to all parties. This process is repeated for
multiple communication rounds.

Transformer Models and BanglaBERT
BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is a type of pre-trained
language model that uses learning to acquire rich contextual representations of text.
BanglaBERT is a BERT based pre-trained model that has been trained using large
amounts of Bangladeshi written text.
As such, it can capture the structural aspects
of the Bangla language and then fine tune the model for specific downstream tasks by at-
taching task specific heads; for example, we attach a token classification head to recognize
medical entities such as Disease, Medicine, and Organ in Bangla clinical text.
Non-IID Data in Federated Learning
In a real-world federated learning environment, the data from one client to the next is
rarely independent and identically distributed; it is non-IID. Take hospitals for exam-
ple: they will have their own mix of patients and diseases as well as distinct ways of
documenting them. Such heterogeneity poses a difficulty for federated training, which is
precisely the sort of issue our system is built to handle with the DP-FedAvg framework.

2.2 Related Works / Existing Systems
Previous studies in federated learning applied to medical NLP are diverse. We review the
existing research in three categories: federated NLP systems, privacy-preserving federated
learning, and Bangla medical NLP.
Federated Learning for Medical Text Classification
Chen et al. [12] present FLCMC, a federated learning approach to Chinese medicinal
text categorization. FedPA and FedPAP - perturbed federated algorithms - were intro-
duced and evaluated on the IMCS-V2 dialogue dataset across simulated non-IID clients.
FedPAP reached a peak accuracy of 0.79, indicating that federated techniques are able
to perform on par with centralized baselines in medical text. However, their paper is
entirely on Chinese text and no differential privacy is used.
Huang et al. [18] proposed a federated ICD-10 classification system with PubMedBERT
across three Taiwanese hospitals as distinct data silos. The federated model achieved
an F1-score of 0.812, a 3.11% improvement over the single-hospital baseline. The study
shows the real-world utility of federated BERT models in the clinic, but the authors admit
that differential privacy had not been included or integrated at the time of writing.
Liu et al. [28] addressed communication efficiency in federated text classification with
FedInitPrune, a parameter pruning algorithm. When applied to a federated TextCNN
setup, this resulted in a 74.26% reduction in communication parameters and a less than
2% decrease in accuracy, representing a significant real-world benefit for resource-limited
hospital settings.
Agrawal et al. [27] performed an extensive systematic literature review of federated
learning for NLP tasks, covering FedAvg, FedProx, FedAdam, LSTM, and BERT-based
methodologies across different datasets. The review highlights key issues such as non-IID
data heterogeneity, communication restrictions, and the inherent privacy–performance
trade-off.
Hasan et al. [19, 2] classified medical prescriptions using an OCR pipeline (EAST detector
with Tesseract) combined with rule-based NLP tagging, reporting 93–95% accuracy on
white-prescription detection; although not federated, the work illustrates the domain-
specific preprocessing challenges typical of clinical text.
Dligach and Miller [9] proposed a two-stage federated NLP pipeline that first learns
11

--- Page 23 ---
patient representations with a Deep Averaging Network and then performs federated
phenotyping, achieving an F1-score statistically equivalent to a centralized baseline on
MIMIC-III and the i2b2 Obesity Challenge dataset, without any differential privacy mech-
anism.
Zhao et al. [35] explored personalized federated text classification through gradient-free
prompt tuning with a genetic algorithm, reducing communication cost by roughly 1000×
relative to gradient-based FL while remaining competitive in accuracy, though at the
cost of heavier local (edge) computation. In the relation-extraction setting, [30] intro-
duced FedED, a federated ensemble-distillation approach for medical relation extraction
that avoids sharing raw gradients between clients, further demonstrating that federated
strategies can be adapted across different medical NLP sub-tasks beyond classification.
Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning
Lyu et al. [31] introduced a privacy-preserving federated learning approach integrating
Local Differential Privacy (LDP), bitwise quantization, and feature hashing for natural
language processing on edge nodes. On sentiment analysis and rating prediction tasks,
they showed that this was possible without costly embeddings, although how well the
approach performs on challenging medical NLP tasks has not been validated.
Ahmed et al. [37] proposed a privacy-preserving edge-aggregated federated model using
Shamir’s Secret Sharing for healthcare severity classification. The proposed hierarchical
aggregation with dynamic edge thresholding showed promising privacy protections on
the MIMIC-III dataset, but the authors acknowledge that the method does not currently
extend to medical text or NLP tasks.
Gawali et al. [4] performed federated deep learning for lung cancer segmentation across 12
hospitals in 8 countries using Personal Health Train infrastructure. Their research shows
that cross-institutional federated learning without data leaving a hospital is practically
possible, but communication lags due to slow hospital connections remain an issue.
Rahman et al. [14] introduced FedHealth-NLP, integrating FedBERT with differential
privacy and a knowledge graph neural network for community health literacy.
Their
method obtained an accuracy of 91.3% across four community health datasets, 4.4%
better than FedAvg+BERT and, at the same time, 42% less communication cost.

--- Page 24 ---
In-Depth FL Evaluation for Biomedical NLP
Shen et al. [29] systematically evaluated FL across eight biomedical NLP corpora, includ-
ing NER and relation extraction, observing that FL models consistently outperformed
single-client training and notably outperformed LLMs (GPT-4, PaLM 2) in the few-shot
prompting scenario as well. Importantly, they identified federated learning with differen-
tial privacy as a research opportunity but did not implement it themselves.
Gao et al. [10] introduced FedCliMask, an ontology-guided semantic masking framework
using UMLS concept hierarchies for clinical NLP. They implemented a federated train-
ing approach in which four levels of semantic masking using ClinicalBERT on synthetic
hospital notes indicated the possibility of meaningful privacy–utility tradeoffs. However,
they did not implement their full federated training pipeline.
General Medical Text Classification and NLP Techniques
Beyond federated and privacy-preserving settings, a broader body of work has studied
medical text classification in the (non-federated) centralized setting, and this line of re-
search informs the modeling choices used in FedMed-BN. [20] compared several deep
learning architectures for classifying unstructured medical notes under varying degrees of
class imbalance, a challenge that is directly relevant to non-IID federated clients. [11] con-
ducted a systematic review of NLP techniques for biomedical document classification, cat-
aloguing feature representations ranging from bag-of-words to transformer embeddings.
[25] applied linguistic-feature-driven supervised deep learning to unstructured medical
text classification, while [3] presented an end-to-end text classification pipeline built on
state-of-the-art NLP models.
Closer to the prescription-processing angle of [19], [26]
performed medical text extraction and classification directly from prescription images,
combining OCR with downstream NLP classification. Taken together, these centralized
studies establish strong non-federated baselines but, like the federated works reviewed
above, none of them jointly address federation, differential privacy, and low-resource lan-
guages.
Bangla Medical NLP
Islam et al. [7] presented BanglaBioMed, the first Bangla biomedical NER corpus, from
BioNLP@ACL 2022. BanglaBERT obtained the best F1-score of 81.33% on this corpus,
13

--- Page 25 ---
covering anatomy, chemicals/drugs, disease/symptom, and medical procedure entities.
The proposed corpus and the performance achieved by BanglaBERT both indicate the
suitability of transformer-based NLP approaches for Bangla biomedical text.
Dhar et al. [38] introduced a hybrid internal feature approach, considering word length
and English-equivalent terms as features for categorizing Bangla medical text documents.
The proposed SGD classifier obtained 97.75% accuracy in binary medical vs. non-medical
classification. This is one of the earliest works in Bangla medical text categorization.

Each of the reviewed techniques has its own set of useful attributes as well as significant
disadvantages. Prescription-based and rule-based NLP systems are very accurate but
can only be applied to their specific domain. Federated phenotyping approaches show
that FL is comparable to centralized performance for clinical text, but it does not come
with privacy-enhancing mechanisms other than data locality. Parallel techniques such as
FedInitPrune can dramatically cut down on transmission expenses, but only in a single
language and without any privacy protection whatsoever. Medical imaging FL systems
16

--- Page 28 ---
like the TB diagnosis framework and the PHT lung cancer study prove feasibility in a real-
world setting, but cannot be applied to text-based NLP tasks across hospital networks.
We also evaluate privacy-preserving frameworks, such as LDP-FL and FedHealth-NLP,
in which differential privacy is combined with federated NLP and evaluated on English
datasets. The extensive biomedical NLP evaluation by Shen et al. also demonstrates that
FL consistently outperforms single-client training and LLMs under few-shot prompting,
and explicitly notes that the integration of DP is an open challenge. On the Bangla side,
BanglaBioMed and Dhar et al. establish strong baselines for Bangla medical NLP, but
neither is equipped with federated learning or privacy protection, making them difficult
to deploy in real multi-institutional settings. Overall, these methods represent good indi-
vidual approaches, but none cover all the needs of differential privacy, federated training,
and low-resource Bangla medical NLP, which is what FedMed-BN addresses.
2.4 Research Gap / Limitations of Existing Methods
The literature review clearly shows a notable research gap. Although federated learning
has been used for medical NLP in English and Chinese, federated learning and differential
privacy have not been combined for Bangla medical text in particular. We were motivated
by the following gaps to develop FedMed-BN:
1. Language gap: All the FL medical NLP systems we reviewed work on English and
Chinese. Bangla, despite having 230 million speakers, does not have any federated
medical NLP framework.
2. Privacy gap: Most federated medical NLP systems do not integrate differential
privacy, which makes them susceptible to gradient leakage attacks.
3. Domain gap: Existing Bangla medical NLP works operate in centralized settings
and have no privacy protection, making them unfeasible for real multi-hospital
deployment.
4. Resource gap: There is currently no system that takes into consideration the
federated learning challenges of a low-resource language like Bangla, specifically in
the medical domain.
17

--- Page 29 ---
FedMed-BN tackles all four gaps by integrating DP-FedAvg with BanglaBERT for privacy-
preserving Bangla medical named entity recognition.

3.2 System Overview / Architecture
This section describes the overall architecture of FedMed-BN. To accommodate several
simulated hospital clients to train a shared BanglaBERT-based medical text classifier
without sharing any raw patient information, the system has been designed as a data
privacy-preserving federated learning pipeline.
3.2.1 High-Level Architecture
At a high level, FedMed-BN consists of five simulated hospital clients (Hospital A through
E) and a central aggregation server. Each client has its own partition of the Bangla med-
ical text dataset, which is non-overlapping. Throughout the round of communications,
clients update their local model using their own data and add Gaussian noise and gra-
dient clipping to protect their information, then return only the differentially private
updates to the central server. These updates are compiled using FedAvg on the server,
and then the aggregated global model is sent back to all the clients. This continues for
T communication rounds.

3.2.3 Design Alternatives and Rationale
In the design stage of the system, multiple architecture options were considered. mBERT
(multilingual BERT) and BanglaBERT were considered as the base language model.
26

--- Page 38 ---
BanglaBERT was chosen because it is pre-trained using a large Bangla text corpus,
which ensures it has a better grasp of the medical vocabulary and Bangla morphology
than generic multilingual models.
Both FedAvg and FedProx were considered for the federated aggregation algorithm. Fe-
dAvg was selected because it is simple and popular, and it has been demonstrated to
work well in non-IID environments where sufficient local training steps can be provided.
Future research could consider FedProx in more extreme data heterogeneity situations.
Two privacy mechanisms were considered: Local Differential Privacy (LDP) and Central
Differential Privacy via the Gaussian Mechanism. The choice of central DP was made
because it achieves better model utility at the same privacy budget, as the noise is only
added once during aggregation rather than once per data point.

4.1 Methodological Framework
FedMed-BN follows a five step methodology that begins with collecting the dataset and
ends with evaluating the trained model. The proposed framework is designed to build a
privacy preserving federated learning system for Bangla medical text classification while
maintaining a fixed privacy budget. The complete workflow of the proposed methodology
is shown in Fig. 4.1. The overall design is inspired by recent studies on federated learning
for healthcare and biomedical natural language processing [22, 15, 38, 33].
Figure 4.1: FedMed-BN Five Step Methodology Framework
The methodology starts by selecting suitable Bangla medical text datasets for training
and evaluation. The primary dataset is the Bangla MedER Multi BERT dataset, which
contains annotated Bangla medical text, while additional supporting datasets are used
28

--- Page 40 ---
to improve the diversity of the data [28, 27, 32]. To simulate a real healthcare environ-
ment, the primary dataset is divided into five separate groups representing five different
hospitals. Each hospital receives a different distribution of medical specialties instead
of identical data. This creates a more realistic setting because hospitals usually focus
on different medical departments and therefore do not contain exactly the same type of
patient records.
After the dataset is divided, the text data is prepared for model training. The Bangla
medical text is cleaned by removing unnecessary characters and formatting errors. The
cleaned text is then tokenized using the BanglaBERT tokenizer so that it can be processed
by the language model. The medical specialty labels are converted into integer class values
because machine learning models require numerical labels during training. Finally, the
processed data of each hospital is divided into training and testing sets, which are used
in the following stages of the framework.
Once the data preparation is complete, the classification model is created. A pretrained
BanglaBERT model available through Hugging Face is selected as the backbone of the
proposed system because it is specifically designed for the Bangla language. A simple
linear classification layer is added to the output of the CLS token.
The size of this
classification layer depends on the total number of medical specialty categories available
in the dataset. The same model architecture is shared by all participating hospitals so
that every client trains an identical model during federated learning [18, 3].
The next part of the methodology performs federated training with differential privacy.
At the beginning of each communication round, the central server sends the latest global
model to all participating hospitals. Each hospital trains the model using only its own
local data without sharing any patient information with the server. During local training,
gradient clipping is applied and calibrated Gaussian noise is added to the gradients before
the updated model parameters are transmitted back to the server. These privacy pre-
serving techniques help reduce the possibility of exposing sensitive medical information
while maintaining model performance. The server collects the updated parameters from
all hospitals and combines them using the Federated Averaging algorithm to generate a
new global model. This process continues for a fixed number of communication rounds
until the training is completed [31, 4, 38, 21, 23].
29

--- Page 41 ---
Finally, the trained global model is evaluated using the testing data from each partic-
ipating hospital. The performance of the model is measured using accuracy, precision,
recall, and F1 score. To study the effect of privacy protection on model performance, the
complete training process is repeated using different privacy budget values represented
by different epsilon values. The experimental results are then compared to observe the
relationship between classification performance and privacy preservation. This evalua-
tion helps determine whether the proposed FedMed BN framework can achieve strong
predictive performance while protecting sensitive medical information [22, 15, 33].

within the current limitations of publicly available Bangla medical NLP resources [12, 33]
4.4 Preprocessing Steps
Raw Bangla medical texts will be preprocessed using the following pipeline before feder-
ated training begins. As the project is still in the report-writing phase, no steps have yet
been implemented, so Figure 4.2 shows each step that is intended.

Figure 4.2: Data Preprocessing Pipeline for FedMed-BN
1. Raw Input: The pipeline will start from the raw Bangla medical text records in the
primary dataset [32] (6,895 observations).
2.
Text Cleaning: Special characters and irrelevant symbols will be removed, and
Unicode normalization will be applied so that the Bangla script is standardized, since
multiple encodings of the same Bangla characters may exist in transcribed or scanned
medical text.
3. Tokenization: The BanglaBERT tokenizer [6] will be used to tokenize each cleaned
text sample, with a maximum length of 128 tokens per sample, padded to a fixed length
for shorter samples and truncated for longer ones, allowing each client to process fixed-
length batches.
4.
Label Encoding: The medical entity labels (such as Disease, Medicine, Organ)
are encoded into integer class IDs for use by the token classification head and the
cross-entropy loss function.
5.

6. Train/Test Split per Client: For each client silo, an 80/20 train/test split will
be used, so that local training and local evaluation always occur on disjoint datasets,
ensuring the final global evaluation in Phase 5 is not influenced by data the model has
already encountered.
4.5 Model Development / Algorithm Design
The core model to be used in FedMed-BN is BanglaBERT (csebuetnlp/banglabert) [6],
fine-tuned for Bangla medical named entity recognition (NER). The choice of BanglaBERT
over general multilingual models (mBERT, XLM-R) is due to the fact that BanglaBERT
is pre-trained on a large Bangla corpus, providing it with a solid pre-trained represen-
tation of Bangla vocabulary and grammar before any fine-tuning on medical datasets
occurs. The model architecture shown in Figure 4.3 reflects the current design, though
it has yet to be implemented or trained.
Figure 4.3: BanglaBERT Model Architecture with Classification Head (Proposed)
The BanglaBERT base model follows the standard transformer encoder architecture [34],
35

--- Page 47 ---
consisting of 12 layers of encoders, 768 hidden dimensions, and 12 attention heads, which
are to be retained as-is when fine-tuned.
The only addition to the architecture is a
dropout layer (p = 0.1) followed by a new linear classification head consisting of layers
that transform each token representation into entity label predictions covering entity
types such as Disease, Medicine, Organ, Hormone, Pharmacological Class, and Common
Medical Terms, culminating in a softmax layer that yields the predicted label. During
the federated training phase, all 12 layers of BanglaBERT along with the newly added
classification head will be fine-tuned for each client, with nothing frozen; the domain
shift from general Bangla text to clinical text is expected to be large enough to warrant
updating the entire network rather than just the classification head. Whether this is
feasible given the available compute (Section ??) will be determined once implementation
starts.
The AdamW optimizer [16] is planned to be used for training, as it is the standard
optimizer for fine-tuning transformer models, with a decoupled weight-decay term that
provides more stable convergence for BERT-style architectures. This optimizer will be
wrapped using the Opacus library [36], which automatically scales per-sample gradients
and applies calibrated Gaussian noise [1] before every local update is uploaded to the
server, thus providing differential privacy guarantees. The following key hyperparameters
are suggested as a starting point:
• Learning rate: 2e-5
• Batch size: 16 samples per local training step
• Local epochs per round: 3
• Total federated rounds: 20
• Gradient clipping norm (C): 1.0
• Noise multiplier (σ): 1.1
• Target privacy budget: ϵ ≤3.0, δ = 1 × 10−5
These values are chosen as starting points for fine-tuning BERT under differential privacy,
based on common ranges reported in the literature [1, 36] for such fine-tuning. They are
not yet the outcome of any experiments conducted by the authors but will be the values
36

--- Page 48 ---
varied in the Phase 5 privacy-utility analysis, once training has been implemented, to
observe how classification accuracy is impacted by increasing or decreasing the privacy
budget.
4.6 System Implementation / Modules Description
The implementation of FedMed-BN is planned as four separate, modular components,
as shown in Figure 4.4. Each module has its own responsibility and can be modified or
replaced without affecting the others, allowing the three team members to work simulta-
neously on different sections of the pipeline once implementation commences. Below are
the module boundaries as planned at the time of writing; none has yet been programmed.
Figure 4.4: FedMed-BN System Modules and Their Planned Responsibilities
Data Module: Will read in all raw data files and produce the five non-IID client silos
after performing the cleaning and tokenization process described in Section 4.4. It will
also produce the 80/20 train-test split for each client and provide a simple data-loader
interface for retrieving train/test chunks.
Model Module: Will implement the BanglaBERT-plus-classification-head architecture
described in Section 4.5 [6], along with the local training loop (forward pass, loss cal-
culation, backward propagation) and a local evaluation function that returns accuracy,
F1-score, precision, and recall over a client’s test split.
37

--- Page 49 ---
FL Module: Built on the Flower (flwr) framework [5], this module will include the
client-side fit() and evaluate() functions for each simulated hospital, as well as the
server-side strategy for coordinating the round schedule and aggregating client weight
updates using FedAvg [17].
DP Module: Will wrap each client’s optimizer using Opacus’s PrivacyEngine [36]
before local training starts.
It is intended to clip each sample individually based on
the configured norm, inject Gaussian noise scaled by the noise multiplier [1], and keep
a running tally of the privacy budget spent so far using Opacus’s built-in accountant,
which can be used to halt or flag training as soon as the target epsilon is likely to be
breached.

Chapter 8
Conclusion and Future Work
8.1 Summary of the Work
The research presented in this paper proposes a new approach called FedMed-BN, an
example of a privacy-preserving federated learning framework for Bangla medical named
entity recognition (NER). At this pre-defense stage, all the work included a comprehensive
review of existing literature on federated learning for medical NLP (Natural Language
Processing), identifying a significant research gap in privacy preserving NLP for the
Bangla language, selecting and validating relevant real world Bangla medical datasets
and designing a full system architecture that incorporates both the BanglaBERT word
embedding model and the DP-FedAvg machine learning algorithm. Ultimately, the goal
of FedMed-BN is to allow a number of hospital client sites to collaborate to train a shared
medical text classifier by enabling them to do so without ever having to share their raw
patient data.
8.2 Key Findings and Contributions
A number of key findings were established during the literature review and system design
phases. First, a critical gap was identified that there are currently no studies that combine
federated learning with differential privacy for Bangla medical text. Second, from the
review of 15 papers on federated learning for medical NLP in languages other than English
or Chinese, it was clear that none of those studies were done in Bangla. As a result of these
findings, FedMed-BN was developed as a unique framework that combined BanglaBERT,
the FedAvg aggregation algorithm and the Gaussian Mechanism as a means of providing
differential privacy. The development of FedMed-BN resulted in the completion of a fully
52

--- Page 64 ---
designed system that consisted of both functional and non-functional requirements, high
level and detail architecture drawings, as well as a preprocessing pipeline that had been
tailored specifically to meet the needs of Bangla medical text.
8.3 Limitations of the Study
Since this represents the pre-defense stage of this project, the implementation and exper-
imental validation aspects have not been accomplished at this time. Currently, the major
limitation associated with FedMed-BN is that the actual results of training a model with
FedMed-BN have not been obtained since the programming and training aspect of the
project have not occurred yet. Moreover, although the primary dataset used to develop
FedMed-BN was a collection of actual Bangla medical documents; due to its relatively
small size, future generalizability of results from experiments using this dataset could
potentially be compromised. Finally, both of these limitations will be addressed in the
final document submitted for CSE 400 (4-2).
8.4 Recommendations and Future Work
Next actions on this project are to implement the DP-FedAvg training pipeline with Py-
Torch, HuggingFace Transformers, Flower and Opacus and then to run experiments over
the five simulated hospital clients and to evaluate the final global model by means of ac-
curacy, F1-score, precision and recall. Future work in the long term will be to incorporate
more heterogeneous Bangla medical text sources into our dataset to improve the general-
izability of the system to other hospitals, extend support to additional entity types and
expand coverage using Bangla BioMed, evaluate more sophisticated privacy techniques
such as Secure Aggregation and work on personalized federated learning methods so as
to improve system performance on non-IID data distributed across hospital clients.

Appendix C
Ethics and Compliance Statements
This research followed standardized ethical guidelines in database construction,
consultation with experts, and privacy protection in system design, from initial design
to final implementation.
Data Sourcing and Licensing
The Bangla-MedER and BanglaBioMed are both publicly available academic corpora
which are both released for research purposes. No proprietary or scraped data, and no
real identifiable patient records were accessed throughout.
No Human Subjects Research
This project is not to be performed on real patients or real hospital data. The federated
“hospital clients” are simulated partitions of the public datasets above, not real
hospitals.
Expert Consultation Ethics
During the requirement analysis (Section 3.1) three practicing physicians were
voluntarily consulted. They were all volunteers who knew the purpose of the
consultation when it took place and who agreed to their information being included in
this thesis. There was no compensation provided and no sensitive personal data was
gathered beyond professional occupation and contact data, which is provided here with
their consent.
62

--- Page 74 ---
Data Privacy by Design
The system is not based on any real patient data, but rather has been designed with
privacy-preserving considerations in mind for later deployment on actual patients: raw
text remains local to each client, and only differentially private model updates
(DP-FedAvg) are sent to the central server, as detailed in Chapter 3.
Compliance Boundaries
This project involves de-identified public data and simulated institutional partitions,
and therefore does not need formal IRB or medical ethics committee approval. If this
were to be deployed in the future with real hospital data, it would also need to be
approved ethically and formally by the hospital where the data is to be shared.
